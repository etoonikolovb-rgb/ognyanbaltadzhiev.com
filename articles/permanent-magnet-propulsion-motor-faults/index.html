The fault you cannot switch off: permanent-magnet propulsion motors

Permanent-magnet and wound-field propulsion motors, compared in daily work and at the moment something goes wrong.

A propulsion motor with a wound field and two winding systems. One winding system drops out on a fault outside the motor. The motor carries on in half-motor mode, on the healthy winding system, at half torque. The ship keeps going.

On a wound-field motor that is close to a routine event. On a permanent-magnet motor the same alarm can mean something very different, depending on where the fault is.

Most large electric propulsion motors at sea are wound-field synchronous motors or induction motors. Permanent-magnet (PM) motors are newer at this size. The ones discussed here are 9.50 MW each, on a dual-fuel cruise ship: direct drive on a fixed-pitch propeller, rated at 130 rpm, with two separate three-phase winding systems. Each winding system has its own converter.

On paper the two motor types do the same job. The difference shows when something fails.

01One difference decides everything

A wound-field motor gets its magnetic field from a current in the rotor. Remove the current and the internal voltage goes with it.

A PM motor has its field built into the rotor. As long as the shaft turns, the stator produces a voltage proportional to speed. The converter can block, every breaker can open: if the propeller turns, the motor is a generator and its terminals are live.

On a ship the propeller does not stop when the power stops. The water keeps it windmilling until the ship slows down.

02In daily work: what the magnets buy

  • Efficiency across the load range. No rotor losses, no excitation power. The efficiency stays high at part load, where propulsion spends most of its time, not only at rated power.
  • Fewer parts. No exciter, no rotating diodes, no slip rings or brushes.
  • Size and weight. Makers quote large savings in space and mass against conventional machines.
  • Power factor close to 1. Less current for the same power, so less converter and cable for the same job.
  • Quiet running. Valued on passenger ships.

03In daily work: what they cost

  • Isolation means stopping the shaft. Opening breakers is not enough. Before a terminal box is opened, the shaft must be stopped and prevented from turning.
  • The magnets set limits. Too much heat or too strong a reverse field can demagnetise them, and the damage is permanent.
  • Maintenance is a specialist job. The rotor pulls hard on any steel nearby. Removing it needs special tools and an authorised workshop.
  • Materials. High-grade magnets depend on rare earths, with the price and supply risk that comes with them.

04In a fault: three cases side by side

SituationWound-field motorPermanent-magnet motor

The breaker between motor and converter opens, because the motor itself feeds the fault from its side. The motor continues on the healthy winding system at half torque.
Internal winding shortThe field is removed. The fault current decays with the field, in seconds. The shaft may keep turning; the ship continues on the other shaft.The field cannot be removed. The magnets feed the fault for as long as the shaft turns. The shaft must be stopped and held.
Emergency stopBreakers open, excitation off.Breakers open. The shaft keeps turning and the motor stays live.

The external fault is a non-event for both: one half of the motor is lost, the ship keeps going. The internal short is where the two designs part ways.

A wound-field field does not vanish instantly. Its time constant is a few seconds, and brushless exciters decay slower, because the rotating diodes cannot drive the field current down. But seconds are enough: once the field is gone, the shaft can turn without feeding anything. Whether it may trail freely, and for how long, is a question of bearings and lubrication and belongs to the maker.

A PM motor needs the shaft stopped. That takes minutes, not seconds, because the ship has to slow down first.

Seconds for the field. Minutes for the ship.

That is the price of the permanent-magnet design.

05What stopping and holding a shaft involves

  1. Slow the ship down. The first call to the bridge is the same as for any serious motor fault: reduce speed, investigate, and stop propulsion on that shaft if conditions allow.
  2. Let the shaft come to rest. With less way on the ship, the windmilling propeller slows down.
  3. Brake and lock. A stopping, turning and locking device on the shaft line brakes the shaft and locks it. The shaft is now motionless.
  4. Resume on the other shaft, with a limit. A locked propeller in the flow sees a torque that grows with ship speed. The brake and the lock have a rated holding torque. So the healthy shaft runs with a speed limit, set by what the locked one can hold.

On the plant discussed here, the automation is built around this sequence. A critical fault in one motor asks for the shaft brake, and the other shaft is reduced automatically, with an override. The design answers the PM problem in the only way the physics allows: by stopping the shaft.

06The questions for the maker

The fault-effect matrix shows the resulting signal, not what triggers it. Three questions are worth asking before delivery, not after the first alarm:

  • How is an internal fault detected? Inter-turn shorts are the most common winding fault in PM machines and the hardest to see early. A few shorted turns can carry a large current and heat locally long before the phase currents look wrong.
  • Can the converter limit the fault current? One known technique short-circuits the faulty phases through the inverter, which limits the current to about rated value instead of letting it run free. Whether a given plant does this is in the maker's design, not in the operating manual.
  • What may the healthy shaft do while the other is held? Speed limit, holding torque of the brake and lock, and the procedure step by step: who does what, from which station.

The trade-off

The magnets pay back every day in efficiency, size and simplicity. They charge for it in two places: isolation, where opening breakers is no longer enough, and the rare internal fault, where the only way out is to stop the shaft, and with it most of the ship's speed.

That is not a reason to avoid PM propulsion. It is a reason to read the fault-effect matrix before the ship sails, and to practise the shaft-stop procedure before it is needed.
