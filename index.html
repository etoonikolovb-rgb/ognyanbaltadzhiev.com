02 / Approach

Four working rules

Learned in engine rooms, applied everywhere else.

Know the source of every number

A setpoint in the manual and the value set in the automation system don't always agree. The one that counts is the one actually set, and a good report says which is which.

Find it in minutes, not hours

During an alarm, every minute spent searching drawings is a minute the bridge waits. Preparation before the alarm is part of the job.

The original stays in charge

Tools index, search and point. The drawing, the maker's manual and the engineer on watch decide.

Standardise for the team on board

Fewer engineers and more ratings in the engine room means clear routines matter more than individual heroics.

05 / Projects

Engineering and study projects

Newest first. Design and research work from TU Varna, and one ongoing personal project.

Personal · 2026 – ongoing · In development

The engine room that answers back

Hundreds of systems, all connected.
A common alarm on a main engine, with more than a dozen possible causes behind a single indication. It comes and goes. While it's active, one of the engine's safety functions may be out of service.
A routine fault, landing on an engineer who joined the ship last week.
A new build that nobody knows yet, and that has to run safely from the first day.

Ships are becoming more integrated, and crews leaner. Every system is documented in detail, but the answer is rarely on one page. It lies in the links between a manual, a wiring diagram and an alarm list, and finding those links can take days.

Experience, engineering intuition and layers of safety still carry every watch. This project adds one more layer: an engineering tool that comprehends a ship's complete documentation, links it across systems and traces a fault from its symptom to its likely causes.

Every finding points back to the original page.
The engineer, the manuals and the drawings stay in charge.

Master's, TU Varna · 2023

DC distribution on ships: ABB Onboard DC Grid™

Course essay on a modular, fault-tolerant DC power system built around electrification: one common DC grid that engines, propulsion drives, batteries and consumers all connect to. It combines different energy sources and propulsion solutions in the most energy-efficient way, and leaves room for new ones as shipping moves to lower emissions. Also covered: solid-state DC circuit breakers and fault protection, cooling, and class guidance (DNV, Korean Register).

Benefits as described by ABB: abb.com, Onboard DC Grid™. Onboard DC Grid is a trademark of ABB.

  1. Lower fuel and maintenance costsDiesel engines run at variable speed, close to their optimal load point: less fuel, less wear, lower emissions, and longer intervals between maintenance.
  2. Space, weight and costLess cabling and fewer conversion stages than an AC plant; ABB quotes a footprint up to 30% smaller.
  3. Different energy sources on one gridBatteries, fuel cells and other DC sources connect with fewer conversion stages, and can be added or changed over the ship's life.
